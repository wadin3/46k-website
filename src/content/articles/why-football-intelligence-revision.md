    heading: "Everyone knows what football intelligence is. Or do they?"
    body: |-
      Game awareness, game understanding, the ability to read the game, a good eye for the game or a high football IQ. Football has plenty of expressions for football intelligence. And we usually understand one another when we use them.

      We probably think of much the same players: Xavi’s overview, Modrić’s ability to control the tempo, Kevin De Bruyne’s timing in decisive passes, Zlatan’s unexpected solutions, Kanté’s pressing, Harry Kane’s ability to find space, Busquets’ composure under pressure, or Pippo Inzaghi’s ability to be in the right place at the right time. Players who seem to be one step ahead and make difficult situations look surprisingly simple.

      But what is it that makes these players so skilful at understanding and solving the game?

      Every player at elite level is technically gifted enough to play a good pass in the build-up, but which qualities enable some players to make their team’s attacking play connect and flow, while others become predictable or get caught in the press?

      Football has developed an extensive vocabulary to describe their qualities: scanning, assessing situations, anticipation, positioning, timing, understanding roles, creativity and decision-making. Other descriptions concern controlling the tempo, staying calm under pressure or playing on instinct.

      These concepts also recur in football’s coaching materials. The English FA, for example, describes successful pressing through six interacting capabilities: scanning, timing, movement, positioning, deception and technique. [[1]](https://www.thefa.com/bootroom/resources/coaching/the-big-six-pressing) UEFA’s coach education emphasises, among other things, positional understanding, opportunities to explore different solutions, and training situations that develop decision-making. [[2]](https://editorial.uefa.com/resources/0267-11ebff960bb7-405a74be27d5-1000/uefa_b_diploma_2020_en.pdf)

      Scouting also emphasises the importance of context. Higinio Vilches, who works with Spain’s youth national teams, describes how players’ technical and tactical qualities must be assessed in relation to their team’s way of playing. [[3]](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/practice/elite-sessions/youth-national-teams/u15-boys-tournament/higinio-vilches-on-scouting-spanish-youth-talent.php)

      Football’s understanding is also beginning to extend beyond visible behaviours. In a presentation for the FIFA Training Centre, researcher Mark Williams links football intelligence to information processing, pattern recognition and the ability to anticipate what is going to happen. Experience plays a central role: skilful players learn which information is worth using. [[4]](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/environment/science-explained/high-performance/play/mark-williams-on-game-intelligence.php)

      Take the player who looks over their shoulder before the ball arrives. The head movement can be observed. But what did the player take in? Could they see the run, keep that information available when their gaze returned to the ball, and notice that the original passing option was disappearing?

      Through research and development work, football has begun to move from observations and experience towards a more systematic understanding of football intelligence. We have become better at describing what skilful players do, and research has begun to investigate the abilities that may lie behind it.

      But how well do we really understand the underlying abilities that make football intelligence possible?

      Can we go even deeper?

    heading: "What lies behind what we see?"
    body: |-
      This is where psychological research becomes interesting. Cognition concerns how we perceive, process and use information. Executive functions help us, among other things, to keep information current, inhibit an inappropriate action, and adapt when circumstances change.

      More and more studies are beginning to investigate the links with football. In a Swedish study from 2012, players in higher divisions performed better on executive-function tests than players in lower divisions. The researchers also found a relationship between test results and the number of goals and assists a player recorded in the following season. [[5]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0034731)

      A Dutch study from 2014 compared 84 academy players with 42 amateur players of a similar age. The academy players were better at stopping a motor impulse, and showed a greater effect of a cue that prepared their attention. [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0091254)

      A meta-analysis of 142 studies across several sports found that more skilful athletes, on average, performed better on cognitive tasks. The differences were larger when the tasks resembled the sport in question. [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000355)

      The research picture contains both promising relationships and clear limitations. The studies above also discuss relationships that could not be established statistically, and predicting future performance is difficult when the evidence base is small and performance measures are hard to define. Football intelligence, as we see it, is far more than the number of goals and assists.

      FIFA also publishes its own material, including a discussion in which Jes Buster Madsen addresses attention, working memory, inhibition, visual information processing, pattern recognition and action selection. The accompanying research article, written with Thomas Habekost and Jacob Ovesen, presents a theoretical model of how players assess a situation, select and execute actions, and use feedback. [[8]](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/environment/expert-knowledge/cognition-in-elite-football.php)[[9]](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1477262)

    heading: "The 46K perspective: Football intelligence begins in the brain"
    body: |-
      At 46K, our starting point is that a player’s way of perceiving and solving the game rests on fundamental cognitive abilities interacting with football knowledge, technique and experience.

      Psychological science gives us concepts with which to investigate that interaction. Working memory, for example, involves keeping information available while we use it. Inhibition helps us resist distractions or stop a response. Cognitive flexibility makes it possible to change perspective and adapt an action. These functions are also relevant to learning and handling new demands. [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143750)

      To connect this perspective with football, we have begun developing our own model of football intelligence.

      In each of its 6 parts, we start from psychological science and formulate our own hypotheses about how the brain’s fundamental functions affect play on the pitch.

      1. ### Perceive the game

         *Where are the ball, teammates and opponents? Where is the pressure coming from? Who is making a run in behind?*

         The player constantly needs to search for and attend to important information. It is not only about seeing what is happening, but also about directing attention appropriately and retaining information even when the gaze moves on.

         A player with a strong ability to perceive the game may find it easier to pick up several important events and maintain an overview under pressure. A player with a weaker ability may miss a run, forget where a teammate is, or become too focused on the ball.

      2. ### Read the game

         *What does what is happening on the pitch mean? A teammate is unmarked, but can a pass reach him? Which space opens up when a defender steps forward? Is the opposition’s back line about to become unbalanced?*

         The player needs to understand how positions and movements relate to and influence one another. This involves recognising patterns, interpreting the situation through previous experience, and quickly updating that understanding as play changes.

         A player with a strong ability to read the game may find it easier to see connections and understand how a movement changes the situation on the pitch. A player with a weaker ability may perceive the same movements, but find it harder to understand what they mean for the game as a whole.

      3. ### Anticipate the next situation

         *Will the forward come towards the ball or run in behind? Is the opponent about to make a pass? Will space open up behind the back line?*

         The player needs to use what is happening now to assess what is likely to happen next. By recognising movements and patterns, the player can prepare for the next situation, while remaining able to change expectations if play takes an unexpected direction.

         A player with a strong ability to anticipate play may begin a run or move into the right position before the situation has arisen. A player with a weaker ability may need to wait until the opponent has already made the pass or the teammate has begun their run, and therefore risks falling a step behind.

      4. ### Find solutions

         *The player on the ball can take on an opponent, turn, play backwards or pass through the opposition’s defence. Off the ball, a player can offer support, stretch the defence or create a new passing lane.*

         The player needs to discover which courses of action are available in the situation. Some solutions are recognised immediately through previous experience, while others require creativity and an ability to rethink when the usual option does not work.

         A player with a strong ability to find solutions may find it easier to see several possible ways forward, even in tight situations. A player with a weaker ability may struggle to come up with alternatives and become stuck with the same type of pass or movement, even though the situation requires something else.

      5. ### Make decisions

         *Should the pass be made immediately or after another touch? Should the defender step forward to intercept or drop back? Is it worth attempting a difficult pass, or is it better to keep the ball?*

         Once the player has identified their options, an action needs to be selected. This means weighing opportunities against risks, deciding what fits the team’s task, and choosing the right moment. Sometimes the player also needs to inhibit an initial impulse or change a decision when the situation changes.

         A player with strong decision-making ability may find it easier to select an appropriate option at the right moment. A player with weaker ability may make hasty decisions, hesitate until the opportunity disappears, or stick with a decision even though the circumstances have changed.

      6. ### Execute

         *The pass needs the right weight and direction. The run needs the right timing. The pressing action needs the right angle.*

         This is where the player’s technical and physical abilities are expressed. The decision must be translated into action, with technique, coordination, speed and strength determining what the player can actually execute.

         Even a wise decision can fail if the player lacks the technical or physical prerequisites to execute it. At the same time, the preceding steps affect how well prepared the player is, which may be crucial to timing and precision, for example.

      The model can be seen as a continuously ongoing cycle. The first five steps can take place in fractions of a second and often interact without the player consciously thinking them through.

      The player’s experience, training and tactical instructions are crucial to how the game is understood and which solutions are available. But throughout the process, cognitive functions underpin everything. They affect which information the player perceives, how it is processed, which possibilities are identified, and which decisions are made.

      Cognition is therefore not a separate part of football intelligence, but part of the foundation for everything that happens before the action.

    heading: "If we can understand it – can we also measure it?"
    body: |-
      Cognitive abilities differ between people. With psychometric tests, we can investigate some of these differences through standardised tasks and systematic comparisons.

      For football, this opens up an opportunity to gain additional information about the player.

      We are particularly interested in what happens when a familiar solution is not enough. A match contains situations that have never been trained in exactly the same form. The player needs to use what they know, perceive what is new, and adapt. Here, we believe fundamental cognitive abilities play an important role.

      Our hypothesis is that a cognitive profile can contribute to understanding both how a player handles the demands of a match and how training and instructions can best be designed. Two players may, for example, need different amounts of structure when learning a new task, or different strategies for solving match situations.

      Research in the area is far from complete, and psychological characteristics are not easy to measure precisely. For example, a study published in 2026 showed that a generic cognitive test battery could distinguish between groups of young elite and sub-elite players, but also showed low to moderate stability on retesting[[12]](https://doi.org/10.1026/2941-7597/a000064). Measuring a cognitive ability and establishing what the result means for a footballer are two tasks that both need to be done properly.

      With this starting point, we are developing 46K Assessment, our upcoming service for psychometric assessment of cognitive and executive abilities. Our ambition is to give players and coaches an understandable profile that can be connected with observations, training situations and development needs.

      We believe the value lies in what the knowledge helps us do: bring out the invisible aspects of players’ talent, understand individual differences, and find more targeted ways to support learning and development.
