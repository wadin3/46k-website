  - kind: text
    heading: Summary
    body: |-
      What sets successful young football players apart? Alongside technique, physical ability and game understanding, researchers are increasingly interested in the brain's executive functions, the mental processes that help us manage information, direct our attention and adapt our actions.

      In a Swedish study, researchers at Karolinska Institutet examined executive functions in young elite football players. On average, the players performed better on two cognitive tests than their peers in the general population. Higher test scores were also associated with more goals scored. [[1]](#reference-1)

      The study supports the idea that both basic and more complex executive functions may matter for success in football during adolescence.
  - kind: text
    heading: What do these cognitive abilities have to do with football?
    body: |-
      Football players have to take in and process information continuously. Where are their teammates? Which spaces are opening up? What are their opponents doing? Which option is best right now?

      All of this happens while players are physically active, under time pressure and in rapidly changing situations.

      Psychologists use the term executive functions for several mental processes that help us meet these demands. They include working memory, impulse control, cognitive flexibility, planning and problem-solving.

      The researchers wanted to know whether these abilities were related to how successful young elite players were on the pitch.

      The players' age was particularly relevant. Executive functions develop throughout childhood and adolescence, but different functions mature at different rates.

      Basic functions develop relatively early. More complex functions, which require several mental processes to work together, continue developing later.

      The researchers therefore wanted to find out whether both types of function already mattered for young players.
  - kind: text
    heading: How was the study conducted?
    visual: vestberg-tests
    body: |-
      The study involved boys from a Swedish elite football academy. They played at the highest level in their respective age groups.

      The researchers recruited 49 players, but the main analysis included the 30 who scored at least one goal during the study period. They were between 12 and 19 years old. [[1]](#reference-1)

      The players completed standardised cognitive tests. Their scores were compared with age-adjusted norms, and the researchers examined associations with goals recorded in the academy's matches.

      Two tests were central to the study.
  - kind: text
    heading: "Working memory: keeping information available"
    body: |-
      The first test was a computer-based task the researchers called Demanding Working Memory (dWM).

      Players saw a sequence of playing cards and had to decide whether a particular card had appeared before. The task required them to retain information from earlier moments while new information was presented.

      The researchers used this task as a measure of basic executive ability.
  - kind: text
    heading: "Design Fluency: finding new solutions"
    body: |-
      The second main test was Design Fluency, from the D-KEFS test battery.

      Participants had to create different designs by drawing lines between dots. They worked under time pressure, and the rules changed between parts of the test.

      To succeed, players had to generate new solutions, avoid repeating earlier responses and adapt as the rules changed.

      The researchers used the test as a measure of more complex executive functions.
  - kind: text
    heading: What did the study find?
    visual: vestberg-results
    body: |-
      On average, players scored above general population norms on both main tests.

      The difference was about half a standard deviation on the working memory task and just under one standard deviation on Design Fluency. [[1]](#reference-1)

      The researchers also found positive associations between scores on each test and the number of goals scored during the study period.

      The association was stronger for the working memory task than for Design Fluency. Combining the two test scores into a single measure produced an even stronger association with goal totals.

      These associations persisted after the researchers accounted for age, height and general intelligence.

      One further result stands out. Players also performed better than general population norms on simpler tests of speed and attention. Scores on those tests showed no statistically significant association with the number of goals scored.

      The cognitive measures therefore differed in how clearly they related to football performance.
  - kind: text
    heading: Why does the distinction between basic and complex functions matter?
    body: |-
      The two main tests place somewhat different demands on participants.

      The working memory task primarily involves keeping information available. Design Fluency also calls for using information flexibly and generating new solutions as the rules change.

      This distinction is relevant when studying adolescents because the brain's more complex control functions continue to develop over a long period.

      One possible interpretation is that basic executive functions matter particularly early in a player's development, while more advanced abilities play a greater role as players get older and the demands of the game increase.

      The same cognitive characteristics may therefore vary in importance over a football career.
  - kind: text
    heading: What does the study add to research on football intelligence?
    body: |-
      Earlier research had shown that adult elite football players scored well on tests of executive functions and that some scores were associated with match performance.

      The contribution of the 2017 study was to examine these associations in young players while distinguishing between basic and more complex executive functions.

      The study adds another perspective on the abilities that may be relevant to understanding football intelligence.

      A player may be technically skilled and physically well developed. Football also involves taking in information, keeping it available and translating it into action.

      Cognitive tests make it possible to study parts of these mental processes separately, outside a match situation.

      This allows researchers to examine those abilities in a more standardised way and compare the results with other relevant measures.
  - kind: text
    heading: Conclusion
    body: |-
      On average, young elite football players performed above the norm on tests of executive functions, and scores on some of those tests were associated with how many goals they scored.

      The most significant point is that these associations extend beyond complex problem-solving to more basic functions such as working memory.

      The study contributes to a broader understanding of the abilities that may matter for success in football and why cognitive characteristics are worth studying in young players.
