  - kind: text
    heading: Begin before the number
    body: >-
      Suppose a club wants to understand why a player finds certain situations difficult.
      Looking for a test immediately might seem appealing. Our proposed starting point is
      different: **first formulate the question** a result is meant to help answer.


      If the question is broad, an individual measure may be difficult to interpret. If it is
      too narrow, a consistent result might still offer little to football. The purpose,
      intended interpretation and uses that should be ruled out all need to be clear.


      For 46K, this is *development work*. We are not claiming that our current tools have
      demonstrated the information the club is seeking. Methods and interpretations need to be
      tested before such use is justified. An attractive interface does not support a stronger
      conclusion. Nor does a longer report necessarily make an uncertain result more useful.

    heading: A study starts a conversation
    body: >-
      [Vestberg and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0034731) examined
      executive functions in footballers, reporting group differences and associations with
      later goals and assists in a subgroup. This offers a concrete reason to investigate
      further.


      Goals and assists do not describe all of football performance. Sampling and methods limit
      how the findings can be used. The study does not validate 46K tests or establish that one
      player's career can be determined by a test score.


      Our interpretation is that the research question deserves care, not a quick product label.
      Which findings hold in new contexts? What additional information is needed? What would
      make us revise an assessment? These are questions a research article should leave with its
      reader. Interest in a finding and attention to its limits can exist together.

  - kind: text
    heading: What would make the information useful?
    body: >-
      In our imagined club, the main question is not who receives the highest score. It is
      whether the information helps player and coach understand something that would otherwise
      be difficult to see. That possibility needs investigation rather than assumption.


      Feedback would need to explain the task, what was actually recorded and which alternative
      interpretations remain. There might also be reasons not to draw an individual conclusion.
      Being able to say that the evidence is insufficient is part of quality.


      We do not want a profile to become another fixed identity. A result from one occasion need
      not be described as the player's essence. It is information from a particular task under
      particular conditions. Any use in development work should be clear, limited and open to
      revision. The result should not outgrow the question that justified collecting it.

  - kind: text
    heading: Feedback that can be questioned
    body: >-
      Imagine a meeting where the player describes the questions the result raises. The coach
      contributes observations. The person responsible for assessment explains its limitations.
      None of these perspectives has to pretend to be a complete picture.


      The meeting might end with a need for more information. It might lead to a cautious
      working idea: a clearer observation during the next training period, or a question to
      revisit. We are proposing a conversation format, not demonstrating an effect of feedback.


      The route back matters too. Who can question the interpretation? What happens when the
      player's experience does not fit our description? How do we explain a decision not to use
      a result? A system that only confirms its own first conclusion is not what we want to
      build.

  - kind: text
    heading: A clearer task for measurement
    body: >-
      A better outcome from development would not necessarily be a longer report. It would be a
      report that answers a bounded question and is open about what remains. Readers should be
      able to distinguish the task's recording, our interpretation and a hypothesis.


      Some uses must therefore wait. If the evidence does not support a conclusion about future
      performance, more confident wording cannot create that support. If the information does
      not help player or coach, we need to ask why it is being collected.


      Can football intelligence be measured? We see questions to investigate separately: which
      abilities, through which tasks, for which purposes and with which limitations? Taking
      those questions seriously is not retreating from the ambition. It is beginning the work
      that the ambition requires.
