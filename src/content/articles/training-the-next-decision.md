  - kind: text
    heading: Choose a moment, not a label
    body: >-
      Start with a situation you can describe without judging the player's character. In our
      example, it is a reception with an opponent approaching from the side. The coach wants to
      understand which options the player notices before and after receiving.


      That is smaller than asking how intelligent the player is. It is also easier to work with.
      Which space was visible? Where was the teammate? What was difficult to notice from that
      position? The player must be able to give an answer the coach did not expect.


      We suggest observing the situation several times before adding instructions. Record what
      actually happens. That creates material for a conversation and may expose a familiar
      assumption. We do not claim that this order of work itself improves performance. It is a
      proposal for making the question more concrete.

  - kind: text
    heading: Make the question playable
    body: >-
      In the next part of the sketch, we keep the ball, direction, teammates and opponents. Any
      change should connect clearly to the question. A coach might explore how the player
      describes available options after a starting position changes.


      Avoid changing five things simultaneously and explaining the outcome through one of them.
      If we introduce a rule, we should be able to say what we hope it makes visible. A more
      complicated exercise is not automatically a more thoughtful one.


      A player may find that a rule makes the situation harder to understand. That is something
      to discuss, not a sign that the player lacks the right ability. Our working idea allows
      the exercise to be adjusted or abandoned. Football is the context for the investigation,
      not scenery around a predetermined explanation.
  - kind: model

    steps:

        text: Return without promising a particular outcome.

  - kind: text
    heading: Ask what became visible
    body: >-
      After a short period of play, the coach can ask a concrete question: which option did you
      notice first? That is more specific than asking why the player made a bad decision. It
      also allows for the coach's view to be incomplete.


      The player might describe a teammate the coach had missed. An instruction may have been
      difficult to reconcile with the situation. The answer might simply be that the player does
      not remember. Our proposed conversation needs to tolerate that too.


      Conversation is not a definitive record of what happened in the brain. It is an
      opportunity to compare perspectives. A new question may be more useful than a confident
      explanation that cannot be challenged. Practical usefulness still needs to be judged in
      the environment where the approach is used.

  - kind: text
    heading: Return to the same question
    body: >-
      The next training session need not begin with another model. Return to the situation you
      selected. What will you observe again? What has changed? Is anything inconsistent with the
      first interpretation?


      Write down what you tried and what you saw rather than immediately grading the exercise.
      More successful actions do not automatically identify which change contributed. Variable
      outcomes do not necessarily make the question uninteresting. Either result requires
      thought.


      This is another reason to be careful about describing a session as cognitive training with
      proven transfer. We have not demonstrated that effect here. We have described how a coach
      and player might make their shared question more concrete, and what they would need to
      acknowledge when assessing the work. It remains a proposal, not a validated intervention.

  - kind: text
    heading: A small step is enough
    body: >-
      The most useful change might be a more precise conversation. From look up to a question
      about what became visible. From bad decision to a description of the options each person
      could actually see. That is our intention, not a reported result.


      Choose a question both player and coach understand. Decide when to return to it. Keep the
      possibility that another question might be better. A model should support thought rather
      than force training into its own shape.


      Development needs football to happen in. For 46K, the ambition is to bring ideas about
      information, conversation and practice together without losing the game. Start where the
      ball is travelling, where the opponent is moving and where the player still has something
      of their own to say.

The first touch is good. The next pass still goes straight into pressure. On the touchline, several instructions suggest themselves: play faster, look up, choose something simpler. But what would we want the player to notice?

We use a fictional training situation to sketch an approach. This is an editorial exercise proposal from 46K, not an evidence-based training programme. Its purpose is to make the development question clear enough to try and discuss.
