  - kind: text
    heading: Same situation, different brains
    body: |-
      Two midfielders receive the ball with an opponent behind them. One moves it on with a single touch. The other controls it, shields it and loses possession. From the touchline, it is tempting to say that the first player thinks faster.

      Rewind one second. Perhaps the first player had already spotted a teammate's run and decided to pass. Perhaps both saw the run, but only one could release the ball from that body position. And if the tactical instruction was to slow the tempo until the team could move up, an extra touch might be deliberate. The situation looks almost identical. The processes behind it may still differ.

      In [the first article on football intelligence](/en/explore/why-football-intelligence/), we described what the player does: perceives the game, reads the game, anticipates the next situation, finds solutions, makes decisions and executes. Here, we go one layer deeper. Which abilities help the player do these things?
  - kind: text
    heading: The brain's toolbox
    body: 'We have chosen eight areas that offer a deeper understanding of what a player needs to manage. Some belong to the so-called executive functions: the processes that help us direct thought and action towards a goal. Others concern how we search for, process and use information. They are not an exhaustive list, nor do they represent eight steps that a player takes in sequence. When the ball arrives, several may be needed at once. [[1]](#reference-1)'
  - kind: text
    heading: Attention and scanning
    body: |-
      Attention determines what takes priority when several things compete to be processed. If you are looking for a friend in the stand, it helps to know which jacket she is wearing. A movement in the corner of your eye may still pull you in another direction. What you are trying to do, what you know about the environment and what stands out within it all help shape what you notice. [[2]](#reference-2)

      Scanning is how we search for information. The gaze can move to a place worth looking at, but attention does not have to follow it exactly. You can keep your eyes still and direct attention to the side. Conversely, something can pass through the middle of your visual field without you noticing. In a classic experiment, many participants missed a person in a gorilla suit while they were busy counting passes. They stayed focused on their task, but that was also why they could miss something else. [[3, 4]](#reference-3)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > Think of a full-back on the far side of a switch of play. The ball is easy to follow. The winger making a run behind the defender is easier to lose. What does the full-back need to look for before the pass is played? A glance over the shoulder may be enough if it comes at the right moment and is directed towards the threat. Match studies show that scanning varies with factors including position and pressure. In one Premier League study, more frequent scanning had a small positive association with successful passes. What interests us is what the player takes away from the glance, and when that information can be used. [[5]](#reference-5)
  - kind: text
    heading: Working memory
    body: |-
      Working memory keeps information available while we use it. In mental arithmetic, the first intermediate result needs to remain available while you work on the next calculation. Working memory can be thought of as a limited workspace where a fresh impression can be used alongside something you already know and the task you are trying to solve. When the content concerns positions and spatial relationships, we call it visuospatial working memory. [[6]](#reference-6)

      How much fits depends partly on how the information can be organised. A familiar abbreviation, such as FIFA, can be held together as a single unit; an unfamiliar letter sequence, such as FNTI, may require each letter to be held separately. Working memory also helps us maintain focus on the goal when other things compete for attention. It is therefore entirely possible to understand an instruction and still lose parts of it while carrying it out. [[6–8]](#reference-6)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > A goalkeeper receives a back-pass. Before looking down at the ball, the goalkeeper has seen that the right-back is free beyond the press. As the ball is controlled, the right-back is no longer in view, but the pass remains an option. This is the kind of moment in which working memory may make a difference: information from an earlier glance must remain usable while attention is needed elsewhere. The more familiar the team's build-up play already is, the fewer separate details the goalkeeper may need to keep track of.
  - kind: text
    heading: Visuospatial ability in motion
    body: |-
      Visuospatial ability is an umbrella term for how we handle locations, distances, directions and spatial relationships. In 46K's work, the emphasis is on the dynamic aspect: what happens to those relationships when what we are tracking moves, and when we change our own position? Distinguishing colour, shape or movement belongs to visual perception in a broad sense. Here, the question is more specific: where, for example, does a defender need to position themselves to intercept a possible pass if the winger continues their run? [[9–11]](#reference-9)

      We can track several moving objects at once. This becomes harder when they move quickly or come close together. We also sometimes have to update our own perspective. When we turn, something that was on the right can end up on the left without having moved. Attention helps us select what to track; working memory retains information that is temporarily out of sight. The spatial work involves keeping track of relationships as the situation changes. [[6, 10, 11]](#reference-6)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > As a cross comes in, a centre-back retreats towards their own goal. One attacker moves towards the near post; another drops behind the defender towards the penalty spot. As the centre-back turns towards the ball, both the distance and the angle to the attackers change. Having seen both players is a start. The challenge is to read which space they are moving into and where the defender's own movement needs to meet theirs. It is this changing geometry that we want to capture with the concept.
  - kind: text
    heading: Processing speed
    body: |-
      Processing speed concerns how quickly mental operations can be carried out, such as recognising or comparing information. When time is short, faster processing may leave room to check one more detail before acting. If processing takes longer, a player may miss information or make the decision too late. [[12]](#reference-12)

      A quick response can arise in several ways, however. The information may be easier to read, the action may be prepared, or the person may act without complete information. Research on simple decisions shows that people can respond faster by prioritising speed at the expense of accuracy. We therefore need to distinguish between having time to use more of the information and simply making the decision earlier. [[13]](#reference-13)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > The team wins the ball in midfield. The ball-carrier drives forward with a teammate on either side. As one defender steps towards the ball, a pass opens up to the right. But the second defender is already moving across to cover. The ball-carrier has a brief moment to judge whether the pass will still get through or whether there is more room to keep carrying the ball.

      Our hypothesis is that processing speed matters for how much of the change a player can take into account before the gap closes. But a well-timed pass may also depend on the player having both defenders in working memory when the first one stepped forward. Some of the information was then already in place when the gap opened.
  - kind: text
    heading: Inhibition
    body: |-
      Inhibition is a broad concept in psychology. Here, the focus is on response inhibition: holding back or stopping an action or impulse that comes readily to mind. A well-practised action is often exactly what we need. The difficulty arises when new information makes the action unsuitable after the impulse has appeared. [[1, 14]](#reference-1)

      An influential model describes inhibition as a race between the processes that initiate an action and those that stop it. When the stop signal arrives late, it becomes harder to cancel the action in time. It is also possible to prepare by holding back from the outset, but an action that should actually be carried out quickly and decisively can then be delayed. [[14]](#reference-14)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > A full-back steps forward when an opponent appears to lose control of the ball. Just before the foot goes in, the opponent nudges the ball inside. The full-back now needs to stop the tackle to retain a chance of following the player and avoid being beaten. The inhibition lies in cancelling the tackle. Which defensive action should follow is the next question.
  - kind: text
    heading: Cognitive flexibility
    body: |-
      Cognitive flexibility is the ability to adapt when what was relevant a moment ago no longer is. Sometimes the goal changes. Sometimes the strategy needs to change. We may notice that the conditions have changed yet remain, for a moment, set on and focused on the old task. [[1, 15]](#reference-1)

      When people switch tasks, the first response is often slower and more error-prone than when the task is repeated. The difference is called the switch cost. Time to prepare for the change can reduce that cost. Flexibility is therefore particularly noticeable when demands change, and less so when the task is stable. Someone who is quick and accurate while the same rule applies may still lose speed precisely when it is time to adapt. [[15]](#reference-15)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > An attacking midfielder makes a run to offer the ball-carrier an option between the lines. An opponent intercepts the pass. Suddenly, the most important space is the one the midfielder has just left: if the opponent reaches it, they can play through the team's press. The player may have plenty of speed in their legs. The transition involves changing the task in mid-run, from becoming available for a pass to taking defensive responsibility.
  - kind: text
    heading: Divergent thinking
    body: |-
      Divergent thinking means generating several possible answers to a task that has more than one solution. At 46K, we use the term generativity for the aspect that interests us most: how quickly a player produces different options that work within the situation's constraints. An idea can be new and effective without being unusual to others. Originality plays an important role in creativity research and in art; for a footballer, the most useful option may be an entirely ordinary action that few others had time to think of. [[16]](#reference-16) Variation matters in football too: three small variations of the same pass offer limited help if they all go through the space the opponent is already covering. [[17]](#reference-17)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > An angled cut-back reaches a striker near goal. A defender blocks the powerful finish towards the near post. The striker could instead guide the ball towards the far corner, turn with it and finish with the other foot, or leave it for a better-placed teammate. All the options share the same constraints, but the player's generativity affects the possibilities of finding an effective finish.
      >
      > Or, more clearly: the striker receives a through ball, the goalkeeper gets there first and heads it out towards the touchline, but the striker realises they can reach the loose ball. Possible solutions include bringing the ball down quickly and hoping to finish before the goalkeeper gets back, or holding possession in the attacking half. Zlatan's solution was a legendary bicycle kick.

      Our hypothesis is that a good flow of ideas can make a difference when the obvious solution does not work and the player needs another before the moment has passed.
  - kind: text
    heading: Planning
    body: |-
      Planning can fit into the moment before a player receives the ball. The player shapes the first touch around what it should make possible with the next. That is the essence of planning: organising actions so that together they lead towards a goal. Research shows that two problems can require the same number of moves but different amounts of foresight. In one, it is possible to start with the step that immediately brings you closer to the goal. In the other, you first need to do something whose value only becomes clear a few moves later. [[1, 18]](#reference-1)

      Planning is a higher-order executive function. We need to retain the goal, think through how the steps fit together and sometimes resist the most tempting first move. Under time pressure, how quickly we produce a workable plan matters. This is different from starting quickly. Someone who starts immediately may need to work out what follows along the way; someone who thinks through two steps before starting can sometimes carry them out without interruption. [[1, 18, 19]](#reference-1)

      > ### On the pitch
      >
      > A midfielder drops deeper in the build-up, but the route beyond the first line of pressure is blocked. A simple sideways pass gives the player time to take up a new position. From there, the ball can be received again at a better angle to play forward. In the penalty area, the same principle can look entirely different. A striker sees that the winger has room to cross. The striker first runs towards the far post, then cuts back towards the penalty spot to create room to finish. In both cases, the first action gains its purpose from what it makes possible afterwards. Our hypothesis is that planning ability helps the player stay one step ahead of the opponent.
  - kind: text
    heading: The same action can have different explanations
    body: |-
      A full-back steps out and is beaten. The reason could be that the winger's body position was noticed too late, that the defender misjudged how quickly they could get there, that the decision to attempt the tackle was difficult to stop despite being late, or that body weight had already shifted onto the wrong foot.

      When the team loses possession, a midfielder may hesitate because it takes time to switch mentally from attack to defence, because their transition responsibility is unclear, or because their legs are tired after several maximal sprints. And a centre-back who loses a runner at a cross may have misjudged threatening angles or spaces, or deliberately prioritised the near post.

      Even a successful action has several possible explanations. A striker who finds a pass instead of taking a blocked shot may have generated several solutions at once, or recognised a situation the team has practised. A midfielder who plays backwards and immediately becomes available again may have planned both steps or only seen the new angle after the pass. Technique, physical qualities, tactical instructions and training background change what is possible. Form on the day, wellbeing and a player's willingness to take risks can also change how the same situation is handled.

      In video analysis, the situations look the same, but to develop something for the next match, we need to know more about why things happened as they did. That is why a good analysis begins by separating what we actually saw from our explanation of it. Two identical clips may then lead to different effective advice for the player.
  - kind: text
    heading: What the research says
    body: |-
      Research on executive functions shows that people can differ in how easily they retain information, stop a response or switch tasks. In a longitudinal twin study, several such individual differences were relatively stable from late adolescence to early adulthood, and genetic factors contributed to that stability. This therefore suggests that these factors are largely innate and difficult to influence [[20, 21]](#reference-20).

      What can be developed also depends on what we mean by training. In working memory training studies, participants often improve on tasks that closely resemble the exact ones they have practised. However, a large meta-analysis found no convincing evidence of broad improvements in areas such as reading, arithmetic or general cognitive ability when compared with active control groups. This therefore suggests that people can improve at a specific task, but that this does not automatically transfer to other tasks that place demands on the same ability, or mean that the underlying ability itself can be trained. [[22]](#reference-22)

      Much therefore suggests that players have different basic capacities for handling information on the pitch. A player can, however, become better at solving a match situation without working memory itself becoming stronger. A midfielder who has practised receiving the ball under pressure may, for example, learn to scan closer to the moment of reception. The player then needs to retain the information for less time before using it. Development therefore comes through finding a strategy that makes the situation easier to handle with the abilities the player already has. [[6]](#reference-6)

      The relationship between basic cognitive abilities and football skill is not clear-cut. In a meta-analysis, sport-specific tasks distinguished more clearly, on average, between skill levels than general cognitive tasks did. This is reasonable: how a player solves a football task also reflects the training they have received, the situations they recognise and what they can do with the ball today. [[23]](#reference-23)

      Our hypothesis at 46K is that basic cognitive abilities may tell us more about a player's development potential than current football performance alone reveals. Two players may solve the same practical task equally well yet have had very different opportunities to practise it. We believe that a cognitive assessment can provide more information about players' maximum capacity, and about how a player can develop their way of reading and solving the game with the right training and support.

      For 46K, the research is promising enough to begin formulating and testing concrete hypotheses. If a winger becomes predictable because of low generativity, we could suggest rotating between three practised dribbles, or scanning for a passing option before receiving the ball if working memory is stronger. We then have something to follow up in the game, rather than a general assessment that the player should think faster.

A one-touch pass looks like a quick decision. But perhaps the most important work had already been done before the ball arrived. What the player has noticed, what is still held in mind and which solutions have been generated are all parts of what we usually call football intelligence.
